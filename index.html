PandaWare

EMG-controlled myoelectric prosthetic hand, built from scratch. PandaWare reads forearm surface EMG, classifies muscle activation in real time on an RP2040, and drives five MG996R servos through a tendon-routed finger mechanism printed in PLA+. A current-sensing safety loop on the INA219 detects object contact and halts grip before force becomes damaging. Total build cost: $118.45, no lab, no machined parts.

Overview

[ 01 ]

Full Mechanical Design

Multiple 3D-printed iterations refining geometry for fit, range of motion, and durability across every finger joint.

[ 02 ]

Custom Circuit Design

Integrated servo control, EMG acquisition, and power sensing on a single hand-assembled system.

[ 03 ]

EMG Signal Pipeline

Adaptive normalization enabling reliable grip detection across varying muscle fatigue and electrode placement.

[ 04 ]

Closed-Loop Safety

Real-time current sensing via INA219 limits grip force automatically, preventing damage to objects. Works reliably on rigid objects; degrades on compliant surfaces.

Measured Performance

$118.45 Total Build Cost
0–12 N Grip Force Range
92% Grip Detection Success Rate
200 ms EMG to Finger Motion Latency
∞ Mechanical Design Iterations
450 mA Contact-Detection Current Threshold

Commercial myoelectric prosthetic hands cost between $20,000 and $100,000. PandaWare was built for $118.45 using consumer components, a hobbyist 3D printer, and no laboratory access. The performance figures above are the honest measured envelope, including where the system underperforms.

Known limitations: The current-sensing grip stop works reliably on rigid objects but degrades on compliant ones, where the current spike at contact is too gradual to detect cleanly. All finger joints are mechanically coupled, so the hand can grasp but cannot form a specific hand configuration. EMG amplitude drifts with electrode placement, sweat, and fatigue; the normalization pipeline reduces this but does not eliminate it.

In design

Precision Characterization Study

How accurately and repeatably can a low-cost tendon-driven hand reach and hold a defined target configuration, and which subsystem limits that precision: the mechanism, the sensing, or the intent decoding? I'm using classical Odissi mudras as the ground-truth pose set, since each mudra is an exactly defined joint-angle configuration that a coupled mechanism physically cannot produce. That makes them a substantially harder benchmark than grasping tasks, and it yields continuous per-joint error data rather than pass/fail on whether an object was picked up.

In design

EMG Assistive Glove (ISEF 2027)

A wearable glove that reads forearm muscle signals and assists people with weak grip. Most assistive gloves leave the thumb passive, which rules out most everyday grasps. This design actuates the thumb in two degrees of freedom, adds force-aware grasping through motor current sensing so delicate objects aren't crushed, and uses flexor-extensor co-contraction to hold flat objects that typically slip out of tendon-driven gloves.

PandaWare v2

PandaWare v1 couples its finger joints, so the fingers curl as a single unit. That is enough to grasp an object and not enough to reach a defined hand configuration. v2 drives each finger joint independently, with three flexor tendons per finger for the MCP, PIP, and DIP joints and passive spring return in place of antagonistic extensors, to remove mechanical coupling as a source of positioning error. It is currently in CAD.

Design Decisions

[ 01 ]

Independent Joint Actuation

Each joint gets its own flexor tendon with passive spring return, so a commanded pose is a set of independent joint angles rather than one shared curl. Dropping antagonistic extensors halves the tendon count and the friction that comes with it.

[ 02 ]

Ball-Bearing Joints

Printed pin joints wear and loosen, so their friction changes from cycle to cycle. Ball bearings at each joint keep friction low and consistent, which is what makes motion repeatable over many cycles.

[ 03 ]

Rigid Skeleton, Compliant Contact

The skeleton is printed in nylon or PETG to hold geometry under tendon load, with TPU 85A on the gripping faces. Stiffness goes where precision matters and compliance goes where the hand touches objects.

[ 04 ]

Per-Joint Angle Sensing

An AS5600 magnetic encoder with a diametric magnet on each joint axis measures the configuration the finger actually reached, instead of inferring it from servo commands. That turns positioning error into something measured directly.

[ Tech Stack ]

Every component chosen for a reason. Here's what powers PandaWare.

[ 01 ] Microcontroller

RP2040

Chosen over an Arduino Uno because the EMG acquisition loop and the servo control loop need to run without blocking each other, and the RP2040's dual cores made that possible at a $4 price point. The tradeoff is no hardware floating-point unit, which constrains how much signal processing can happen on-device.

[ 02 ] Firmware

MicroPython

Chosen for iteration speed. Being able to change a filter constant and re-run immediately mattered more than raw execution speed during development, since the bottleneck was mechanical settling time rather than compute.

[ 03 ] EMG Sensor

MyoWare 2.0

Surface EMG was the only viable intent signal available without implanted hardware or a lab budget. The core problem it creates is that its output drifts with electrode placement, skin impedance, sweat, and muscle fatigue, which is what drove the custom normalization pipeline below.

[ 04 ] PWM Driver

PCA9685

Offloads servo timing to dedicated hardware over I²C, freeing the microcontroller to sample EMG continuously rather than interleaving PWM generation with acquisition. Drives all finger servos from two pins.

[ 05 ] Current Sensor

INA219

This is the component the whole grip-safety system depends on, and it was chosen because fingertip force sensors were outside budget. Instead of measuring force directly, the system infers object contact from the spike in servo current draw when a finger stalls against something. It works well on rigid objects and degrades on compliant ones, which is the exact relationship I'm now characterizing formally in a follow-up study.

[ 06 ] Actuators

MG996R Servos

High-torque metal-gear servos chosen for holding position under load rather than for speed. One servo per finger, driving tendon cables through the printed finger channels.

[ 07 ] Fabrication

FDM 3D Printing

Printed in PLA+ on a partially broken consumer printer, which meant designing around inconsistent tolerances rather than assuming them. Wall thickness and pin-joint reinforcement were sized for print reliability, not just for strength.

[ 08 ] Mechanism

Tendon Actuation

Tendon routing was chosen because it moves the actuators off the fingers and into the palm, which is the only way to keep finger geometry close to human scale. The cost is friction and slack accumulating along the tendon path, which sets a hard ceiling on positioning precision, which is the limitation that motivates the second-generation design.
